Privacy, access & safety.
What the sources say.

LAST REVIEWED · 21 OCTOBER 2026

A source-linked briefing on selected internet law and policy. Legal claims below are limited to the named jurisdiction; campaign goals and policy arguments are identified as such rather than stated as law. Sources were checked on 21 October 2026.

✦EUROPEAN UNION · AI
APPLICATION DATES VARY

The EU AI Act moves into application

The European Commission states that the AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and generally applies from 2 August 2026. Some provisions have separate dates: the Commission lists 2 December 2027 for certain high-risk use cases and 2 August 2028 for high-risk AI in regulated products.

WHAT THE LAW DOES

The Act uses a risk-based framework and sets obligations for prohibited practices, general-purpose AI, and high-risk systems.

SCOPE NOTE

Not every AI system has the same obligations or start date. Consult the official timeline for the applicable category.

Source: European Commission, AI Act timeline ↗
◎EUROPEAN UNION · PLATFORMS
EU REGULATION

Digital Services Act: platform duties and user options

The European Commission says the DSA requires platforms to give reasons for certain content-removal decisions and provide ways to appeal. It also sets rules for reporting illegal content and ad transparency; very large platforms must offer a non-personalised feed option.

REQUIREMENTS LISTED BY THE COMMISSION

The DSA includes user notices and appeals, reporting channels, ad disclosures, and additional duties for very large platforms.

SCOPE NOTE

Specific duties vary by service type and size. The Commission’s overview describes the EU rules and their scope.

Source: European Commission, DSA overview ↗
⌕CALIFORNIA · PERSONAL DATA
CALIFORNIA · CCPA/CPRA

California privacy requests and opt-out rights

The California Privacy Protection Agency says eligible consumers may have rights to know, delete, or correct personal information and to opt out of certain sale or sharing. The CPPA FAQ describes request methods and Global Privacy Control opt-out signals.

OFFICIAL REQUEST ROUTES

The CPPA advises consumers to use the request methods in the business’s privacy policy and describes when covered businesses must honor qualifying opt-out preference signals.

SCOPE NOTE

These are California rules; applicability, exceptions, and request procedures depend on the law and business involved.

Source: California Privacy Protection Agency FAQ ↗
ACCESS, OWNERSHIP & PRESERVATION

These briefs distinguish legal rules from campaign aims and arguments made by named organizations.

⇄COPYRIGHT · ACCESS
COPYRIGHT · U.S. EXAMPLE

Piracy: U.S. copyright rules on unauthorized copying

This brief concerns unauthorized copying or distribution of copyrighted works. Under U.S. law, copyright protects qualifying original works fixed in a tangible medium. The law also sets limitations and exceptions; whether a specific use is lawful depends on the facts and applicable law.

STATUTORY FACT

The U.S. Copyright Office states that copyright protects expression, not ideas. U.S. law includes limitations such as fair use.

SCOPE NOTE

This is a U.S. example. Copyright terms, exceptions, and enforcement differ across jurisdictions.

Source: U.S. Copyright Office, copyright basics ↗ Source: U.S. Copyright Act, §107 (fair use) ↗
◇DIGITAL GOODS · OWNERSHIP
EU · DIRECTIVE 2019/770

EU rules for digital content contracts

The EU Digital Content Directive (2019/770) sets rules for certain contracts supplying digital content or digital services to consumers, including conformity requirements and remedies. It does not establish a single worldwide rule that every digital purchase has the same ownership terms.

WHAT THE DIRECTIVE COVERS

EU member states must apply its rules to contracts within its scope, subject to the directive’s definitions and exclusions.

SCOPE NOTE

The directive is not a general statement of ownership for every product or jurisdiction. Check the contract and your local consumer law.

Source: EUR-Lex, Directive (EU) 2019/770 ↗
🎮GAMES · STOP KILLING GAMES
CAMPAIGN · NOT A LAW

Stop Destroying Videogames: campaign and initiative

Stop Killing Games is a campaign about video-game shutdowns. The related European Citizens’ Initiative is titled “Stop Destroying Videogames.” An initiative can invite the European Commission to consider action; the Commission is not obliged to propose legislation, and a proposal must be adopted by the relevant EU institutions to become law. This briefing does not claim the initiative’s current signature or Commission-response status.

CAMPAIGN POSITION

The initiative’s stated objective concerns rules for video games that become unplayable after a publisher ends support.

LEGAL STATUS

A citizens’ initiative is not legislation. The official initiative tracker records its status; the Commission explains the EU process.

Source: EU initiative, “Stop Destroying Videogames” ↗ Source: European Commission, how initiatives work ↗ Campaign source: Stop Killing Games ↗
▤ARCHIVES · PRESERVATION
U.S. COPYRIGHT ACT · §108

U.S. copyright rules for library and archive copies

U.S. Copyright Act §108 allows qualifying libraries and archives to make certain copies under specified conditions. The statute includes limits on number, purpose, access, and commercial availability; it is not a general permission to copy or distribute any work.

WHAT THE STATUTE SAYS

Section 108 includes provisions for preservation and replacement copies, subject to the requirements in the statute.

SCOPE NOTE

Section 108 is U.S. law and applies only when its conditions are met. Other countries have different rules.

Source: U.S. Copyright Act, §108 ↗

CIVIC PARTICIPATION

Ways to submit views on policy.

01

Identify the relevant body. Depending on jurisdiction and topic, this may be an elected representative, regulator, or consumer-protection agency.

02

Check for an open consultation. Public consultations can provide a formal route to comment. Check the relevant official notice for who may comment, how to submit, and the deadline.

03

Use official processes. Submit comments through the named channel, contact representatives, or use the regulator’s complaint process where available.

Participation routes, deadlines, regulator powers, and complaint processes vary by jurisdiction. For the EU citizens’ initiative process described above, see the European Commission’s official explanation.
